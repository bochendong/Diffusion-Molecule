\begin{abstract}
Goal-directed molecular design is central to drug discovery and materials
development, yet practical design requests often combine a variable number of
numerical objectives and may either require a molecule to be created from
scratch or an existing molecule to be modified. Existing systems commonly
treat de novo generation and molecular editing as separate tasks, encode
multiple objectives as a flat condition or free-form instruction, and report
property-selected best-of-many outputs. We introduce \emph{MolProgram}, a
unified molecular transition framework in which a design request is represented
as a variable-length program of property targets, tolerances, and directions,
while the molecular input is represented only by its initial graph state.
An automatically derived initial-state token distinguishes an empty graph from
a supplied source graph without routing to a task-specific model. A single
autoregressive policy, checkpoint, typed graph-action vocabulary, and
deterministic interpreter are consequently used for both generation and
editing. MolProgram combines frozen LLM-derived instruction features with
explicit numerical program tokens, emphasizes high-order programs through a
property-count curriculum, and applies group-relative reinforcement learning
with programmatically verifiable molecular feedback. The model constructs
molecules without retrieval from an external molecular library or
property-aware inference-time reranking. In a representation audit, the common
transition language covers 96.5\% of mixed de novo and editing trajectories and
reconstructs 99.8\% of the covered editing targets. On direct de novo
generation, group-relative post-training improves Pass@8 and Pass@20 over
supervised fine-tuning by 6.35 and 7.64 percentage points on two-to-seven
property programs, and by 3.90 and 6.50 points on out-of-distribution programs.
A first shared-policy gate obtains 26.5\% macro strict editing accuracy on its
first candidates but fails on long-horizon 6p/7p construction, identifying
sequence horizon as a central obstacle to unified learning.
These results motivate molecular design as one compositional transition problem
while identifying the optimization required to make both boundary conditions
work in a single policy.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Goal-directed molecular design is widely used in drug discovery and materials
development to create or modify molecules toward desired physicochemical and
biological profiles. It is commonly formulated as a conditional molecular
transition problem: given a property program
$P=\{(j,y_j,\tau_j,d_j)\}_{j=1}^{m}$ and an initial molecular graph $G_0$,
learn a policy $\pi_\theta$ whose terminal graph satisfies all clauses in $P$.
Each clause specifies a property identity $j$, target $y_j$, tolerance
$\tau_j$, and optional direction $d_j$; $G_0=\varnothing$ defines de novo
generation, whereas $G_0=G_{\mathrm{src}}$ defines source-conditioned editing.
A general solution iteratively samples a molecular action
$a_t\sim\pi_\theta(a_t\mid P,G_t,a_{<t})$ and applies an interpreter
$G_{t+1}=\mathcal I(G_t,a_t)$ until a stop action produces the candidate
molecule.

Existing approaches instantiate this iteration through several broad
paradigms rather than isolated model families. Sequence generators decode
molecular strings token by token and provide a compact route to conditional
generation \citep{bagal2022molgpt}. Graph and image diffusion models iteratively
denoise structural states and can impose numerical or categorical guidance
throughout construction \citep{liu2024graphdit,wang2025sketchmol}.
Arbitrary-subset conditional models use property masking, guidance, or
self-criticism to operate on different subsets of available objectives
\citep{jolicoeurmartineau2024stggplus}. Instruction-tuned molecular language
models use natural-language task descriptions to transfer across objectives
and molecular operations \citep{dey2025gellm3o,dey2025controllable}. In
parallel, verifier-guided sampling and reinforcement learning use molecular
predictors or programmatic property functions to improve outcomes that are not
fully captured by supervised likelihood
\citep{wang2025sketchmol,gao2026cmoral,shao2024deepseekmath}.

These paradigms all approximate a sequence of condition-dependent molecular
transitions; however, their interfaces make compositional and operational
generalization difficult to identify. A flat property vector does not expose
the internal structure or length of a variable objective set, making
high-order composition difficult to distinguish from interpolation among
individual property correlations. A free-form instruction provides semantic
flexibility but is imprecise about numerical targets, tolerances, and reversed
or extreme constraints. Direct string generation is efficient but fragile
under long-horizon validity and source preservation, whereas graph construction
is valid by design but often tied to a fixed task interface. Most importantly,
de novo generation and molecular editing are usually implemented with separate
heads, decoders, action spaces, or post-processing pipelines, even though they
differ only in whether the initial graph is empty. Best-of-many evaluation can
then hide these modeling limitations by increasing success through search or
property-aware selection rather than by increasing the policy probability of a
strict solution \citep{gao2022sampleefficiency}.

Our key observation is that generation and editing need not be unified by a
task router; they can instead be treated as executions of the same molecular
transition program under different boundary conditions. If the objective is
represented as an explicit variable-length program and graph occupancy is
represented as part of the initial state, one policy can learn both how
constraints compose and how the available molecular state changes the next
valid action. This formulation preserves the semantic benefit of an
instruction, the numerical precision of structured targets, and the chemical
validity of executable graph transitions.

Thus, we present \textbf{MolProgram}, a unified molecular transition framework
that combines LLM-derived instruction conditioning, explicit property-program
tokens, an automatically derived initial-graph token, typed graph actions, and
group-relative reinforcement learning. MolProgram first augments frozen
instruction features with deterministic tokens for every active property
clause and with an \textsc{Init-Empty} or \textsc{Init-Source} observation
computed directly from $G_0$. A single compact autoregressive Transformer then
emits a variable-length program over atom initialization, atom and bond
addition, local replacement, deletion, fragment attachment, bond-order change,
and stop actions. One deterministic interpreter executes these actions on
$G_0$, so de novo generation and editing share the same decoder, checkpoint,
action vocabulary, and output semantics. Supervised training uses a
property-count curriculum to emphasize high-order programs, after which a
GRPO-style objective compares groups of executions for the same program and
updates the common molecular policy using validity, property satisfaction, and
source-relative feedback; the LLM feature extractor remains frozen.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We formulate de novo generation and molecular editing as one
  initial-state-conditioned transition problem and introduce MolProgram, which
  uses one property-program interface, policy, typed action language, checkpoint,
  and interpreter for both operations without a task router or external
  molecular library.
  \item We develop property-count curriculum learning, verifier-based
  group-relative post-training, and a budget-transparent evaluation that
  separately reports raw success, Pass@$k$, validity, uniqueness, and
  source-preserving editing, providing empirical evidence for improved
  high-order and out-of-distribution control while retaining explicit failure
  cases.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews
conditional molecular generation, editing, and reinforcement post-training.
Section 3 formalizes compositional property programs and the unified molecular
transition problem. Section 4 presents MolProgram and its training objectives.
Section 5 reports de novo, editing, complexity-scaling, and
out-of-distribution results. Section 6 discusses limitations and broader
implications, and Section 7 concludes the paper.
