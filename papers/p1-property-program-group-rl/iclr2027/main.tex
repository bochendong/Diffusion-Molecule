\begin{abstract}
Property-guided molecular design asks either for a new molecule satisfying a
specification or for a source molecule to be modified under the same kind of
constraints. Although these regimes share an objective language, they are
usually handled by separate generators, while property-aware best-of-many
selection can obscure how much strict success is assigned by the model itself.
We introduce \emph{MolProgram}, a unified molecular language model conditioned
on a variable-length property program and an optional source molecule. One
Qwen2.5-VL-7B base, LoRA adapter, tokenizer, prompt template, and JSON response
schema performs both de novo construction and source-conditioned editing; an
empty source requests construction and a canonical source SMILES requests
editing, without a learned router or mode-specific head. We first stabilize the
shared policy with validity-aware multi-negative supervision. We then apply a
critic-free group-relative update that compares four raw molecules for the same
request using a programmatic reward for validity, clause satisfaction, the
worst-satisfied clause, and, only for editing, source fidelity. An SFT anchor
limits drift, while target molecules, external molecule libraries, benchmark
rows, and property-aware inference-time reranking are excluded from the policy
update. Before group-relative post-training, the frozen unified checkpoint
raises MolEdit-Instruct strict
$\operatorname{AnyAcc}_{\rm all}^{@8}(.65)$ from 0.21 to 0.36 and obtains
best-of-40 de novo success of 0.79, 0.65, and 0.46 on two-, three-, and
four-property programs. At the same time, its raw candidate success remains
low and its six-/seven-property results expose substantial headroom---the
specific gap targeted by group-relative post-training. MolProgram therefore
turns construction and editing into one auditable policy-learning problem,
while keeping benchmark-specific verification and sampling budgets explicit.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Goal-directed molecular design is widely used in drug discovery and materials
development to construct new compounds or modify existing ones toward desired
physicochemical and biological profiles. These regimes differ in their boundary
conditions but share the same semantic question: which molecular structure
satisfies a requested set of constraints? Given a property specification
$P=\{(j_\ell,g_\ell)\}_{\ell=1}^{m}$, an optional initial molecular graph
$G_0$, and an external evaluation contract $B$, we seek
\begin{equation}
  G^\star\in\argmax_{G\in\mathcal F(G_0)} V_B(P,G;G_0),
  \label{eq:intro-design-problem}
\end{equation}
where each $j_\ell$ identifies a property, $g_\ell$ is a numerical or
directional goal, $V_B$ measures satisfaction under benchmark-defined
tolerances and source-fidelity rules, and
$\mathcal F(G_0)$ is the family of chemically admissible outcomes reachable
from the initial state. The variable-length list $P$ is serialized directly in
the model input; benchmark tolerances remain part of $B$ rather than being
silently absorbed into the model. $G_0=\varnothing$ defines de novo
construction, whereas $G_0=G_{\mathrm{src}}$ additionally imposes a
source-preserving editing boundary. In this work, both cases use one molecular
language model,
$s\sim\pi_\theta(s\mid P,G_0)$, which emits a complete molecular string. The
boundary is expressed in the input rather than by selecting a separate model:
the source field contains either an explicit empty symbol or a canonical source
SMILES. The verifier $V_B$, however, remains boundary-aware because editing must
also measure fidelity to $G_{\mathrm{src}}$. This distinction---one shared
generative model with benchmark-specific external verification---defines the
scope of unification studied here.

Research on this problem has developed several complementary solution
paradigms.  Autoregressive molecular generators factorize the conditional
distribution into token-level decisions, giving a compact and efficient route
to global construction \citep{bagal2022molgpt}.  Graph and image diffusion
methods instead refine a noisy structural state through repeated denoising,
allowing property information to guide both local structure and global layout
\citep{liu2024graphdit,wang2025sketchmol}.  Arbitrary-subset conditional models
use property masking, guidance, or self-criticism to vary which objectives are
active without training a separate generator for every subset
\citep{jolicoeurmartineau2024stggplus}.  Source-conditioned optimization
methods cast design as molecular translation, latent-space movement, or local
graph transformation, thereby biasing the transition toward preservation of a
given molecule \citep{irwin2022chemformer,zhuang2026moleditrl}.  Instruction
tuning replaces a fixed task interface with natural-language specifications,
supporting transfer across property combinations and molecular operations
\citep{dey2025gellm3o,dey2025controllable}.  Verifier-guided sampling and
reinforcement learning instead turn molecular predictors or deterministic
property functions into outcome-level feedback for non-differentiable design
objectives \citep{wang2025sketchmol,gao2026cmoral}.

Despite their different representations, these paradigms all learn a
condition-dependent distribution over molecular outcomes. However, a flat
property vector suppresses the length and clause structure of the objective,
making genuine high-order composition difficult to distinguish from
interpolation among correlated properties.  Free-form instructions provide a
flexible interface but do not by themselves specify numerical targets,
tolerances, reversed directions, or extreme values precisely. At the system
level, construction and editing are therefore often implemented with separate
models, adapters, action spaces, or routing rules. This makes it difficult to
determine whether knowledge learned for one regime transfers to the other, and
it multiplies deployment and maintenance costs. At the opposite extreme,
calling an entire pipeline ``unified'' can hide task-specific property oracles,
source-similarity checks, and best-of-many selection behind the model boundary.
Property-selected evaluation is especially important: success can rise simply
by increasing inference-time search, even when the probability of a strict raw
solution remains small \citep{gao2022sampleefficiency}.

Our key observation is that a useful first step toward unification is narrower
and more testable than a fully end-to-end system: construction and editing can
share the \emph{same learned molecular distribution} while retaining explicit,
auditable task boundaries around verification and candidate selection. The
same compositional property program specifies the requested outcome in both
regimes, and an optional source molecule supplies the additional editing
context. This formulation tests whether one set of language-model parameters
can acquire both construction and source-conditioned modification behavior,
without requiring the property oracle or benchmark protocol to become part of
the neural model.

Following this view, we present \textbf{MolProgram}, a single-checkpoint
molecular language model for de novo generation and source-conditioned editing.
The implementation uses one Qwen2.5-VL-7B base model, one LoRA adapter, one
tokenizer, one prompt template, and one response schema of the form
\texttt{\{"plan":"BUILD|MODIFY", "smiles":"..."\}}. De novo examples
provide \texttt{source=<EMPTY>}; editing examples provide a canonical source
SMILES. There is no learned router, mode-specific adapter, or mode-specific
output head. Both regimes are mixed in the same supervised continuation, with
balanced chosen-completion likelihood and shared molecular syntax.

Simple mixed supervision is not sufficient because the two regimes expose
different failure modes. In particular, an editing model may learn to copy its
source, while stronger anti-copy pressure can reduce chemical validity. We
therefore use validity-aware multi-negative contrastive supervision. For both
regimes, the model compares the chosen molecule with an invalid-SMILES
corruption and with a valid molecule paired to the wrong condition. Editing
examples additionally include the unchanged source as a weak negative. These
small margins train one checkpoint to recognize chemical validity, condition
compatibility, and nontrivial modification without introducing separate task
heads. We describe this objective as contrastive SFT with ORPO-like margins,
not as a full preference-optimization algorithm.

Contrastive supervision addresses representation-level failures, but it does
not directly optimize the joint event used at evaluation. This matters in the
current checkpoint: strict solutions often appear only after several samples,
indicating that the policy already contains useful molecules but assigns too
little probability to them. Our second insight is to turn this best-of-$K$
headroom into a within-request learning signal. For each property program, we
sample a group of four raw completions and score them with one programmatic
reward. The shared part rewards chemical validity, average clause satisfaction,
the least-satisfied clause, and joint success; the editing boundary adds source
similarity and penalizes an unchanged copy. Group-normalized rewards weight the
completion log probabilities, and a chosen-completion SFT anchor limits drift.
No target molecule is exposed to the reward, and no benchmark row or static
molecule pool enters training.

Thus, MolProgram combines an optional-source property language with
verifier-aligned group-relative post-training of one molecular policy. The
update is critic-free and uses within-group standardized advantages, but does
not use PPO ratio clipping or a reference-model KL term; we therefore call it a
\emph{group-relative policy update} rather than claiming an exact reproduction
of every GRPO implementation. This distinction leaves the central hypothesis
precise: outcome-level relative feedback should move probability mass from
partially satisfying or rare best-of-many molecules toward raw strict success,
without sacrificing the shared checkpoint or chemical validity.

Our evaluation keeps the system boundary visible. The model generates an
ordered raw candidate stream without target access. De novo candidates are
then scored by deterministic property functions; the matched SketchMol result
uses its declared 40-candidate, property-aware finalizer. Editing candidates
are assessed with property direction and source-similarity criteria native to
MolEdit-Instruct. We report raw and selected metrics separately and do not pool
the two benchmark families into a synthetic unified score. Thus, ``unified''
in this paper refers to the learned checkpoint and interface, not to an
end-to-end replacement of benchmark-specific verification.

The results show both the value and the cost of this intermediate design point.
Before the group-relative update, the validity-aware unified continuation
improves strict editing
$\operatorname{AnyAcc}_{\rm all}^{@8}(.65)$ from 0.21 to 0.36, with a
paired-bootstrap 95\% interval of $[0.07,0.24]$ for the improvement. On a
locked pilot of 100 conditions per property count, the same
checkpoint reaches 0.79, 0.65, and 0.46 strict success for two-, three-, and
four-property de novo programs under the matched 40-candidate finalizer. It is
slightly worse than its predecessor on the first two strata and better on the
four-property stratum; neither checkpoint establishes an advantage on the
expanded hard six-/seven-property audit. Single-checkpoint sharing is therefore
feasible, but raw candidate success and hard-composition success remain low.
These frozen results establish the pre-RL baseline and motivate the
group-relative continuation; its preregistered row is left unfilled until the
locked evaluation completes.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We formulate molecular construction and source-conditioned editing as
  one optional-source property-program problem and instantiate it with a single
  molecular language-model checkpoint and strict output interface, without a
  learned task router, mode-specific head, or external molecule materializer.
  \item We combine validity-aware multi-negative initialization with a
  verifier-aligned group-relative policy update and a budget-transparent
  evaluation. The design directly targets raw joint property success while
  making source fidelity, external verification, and best-of-many selection
  visible rather than folding them into the unification claim.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Conditional Molecular Construction}
Conditional molecular generation constructs a molecule from an empty state
under one or more desired properties. Autoregressive models condition SMILES
decoders on property values or scaffolds \citep{bagal2022molgpt}, while SPMM
learns a bidirectional interface between molecular strings and property vectors
\citep{chang2024spmm}. More recent sequence approaches use richer latent
conditions: the Latent Prompt Transformer jointly models molecules and target
properties and performs posterior sampling over a learned prompt
\citep{kong2024lpt}. Structure-aware alternatives operate on graphs, spanning
trees, three-dimensional geometry, or molecular images. Graph DiT injects
numerical and categorical conditions into graph diffusion
\citep{liu2024graphdit}; Diffusion Twigs co-evolves structural and property
processes \citep{mercatali2024twigs}; STGG+ combines arbitrary-property masking
with self-criticism \citep{jolicoeurmartineau2024stggplus}; and GeoRCG uses an
intermediate geometric representation to guide equivariant generation
\citep{li2025georcg}. SketchMol instead unifies construction and mask-based
editing through molecular images and expert feedback \citep{wang2025sketchmol}.
These methods establish strong conditional generators, but their objectives are
usually represented as fixed vectors, masked property sets, latent prompts, or
free-form instructions. MolProgram makes the number and internal structure of
the requested controls explicit through variable-length property--goal clauses,
while the benchmark contract supplies tolerances. Its ordered-budget
evaluation also separates compositional difficulty from the additional search
provided by best-of-many sampling.

\subsection{Source-Conditioned Molecular Editing}
Molecular editing starts from a source graph and seeks property improvement
without discarding its useful structure. Rule-based methods apply matched
molecular-pair transformations, fragment substitutions, or bond-edit templates;
they are chemically interpretable but are limited by the coverage of their
transformation libraries \citep{hussain2010mmp}. Latent and hierarchical graph
models instead encode a molecule and decode a nearby structure. JT-VAE builds
molecules from valid substructures, hierarchical graph-to-graph models decode
motifs from coarse to fine, and Modof restricts optimization to a learned
fragment modification \citep{jin2018jtvae,jin2020hierarchical,chen2021modof}.
Such structural biases improve locality, although latent compression and a
fixed edit vocabulary can restrict precise control over diverse objectives.
Sequence and language models formulate editing as molecular translation or
instruction following. Chemformer pretrains a SMILES-to-SMILES Transformer
\citep{irwin2022chemformer}; MolGen uses SELFIES pretraining and chemical
feedback \citep{fang2024molgen}; and MoleculeSTM aligns structures and text for
open-vocabulary retrieval and zero-shot editing \citep{liu2023moleculestm}.
Prompt-MolOpt and DrugAssist learn prompt- or dialogue-conditioned molecular
transformations \citep{wu2024promptmolopt,ye2025drugassist}. GeLLM3O and
GeLLM4O-C scale instruction tuning to multi-property and property-specific
objectives \citep{dey2025gellm3o,dey2025controllable}, while AgentDrug places an
LLM inside a toolkit-validated refinement loop \citep{le2025agentdrug}. These
interfaces are flexible, but token-level proximity is not equivalent to
graph-level fidelity. MolEditRL directly addresses this mismatch with
source-conditioned discrete graph diffusion and editing-aware reinforcement
learning \citep{zhuang2026moleditrl}. MolProgram instead supplies the source
SMILES to the same causal language model used for de novo construction. Its
locality bias is learned from paired targets and a weak anti-copy contrastive
term rather than from a separate graph-edit action space.

\subsection{Verifier-Guided Optimization and Reinforcement Learning}
Reinforcement learning provides a direct interface to non-differentiable
chemical objectives. REINVENT adapts a pretrained SMILES policy with a
property-scoring function \citep{olivecrona2017reinvent}, whereas GCPN casts
atom and bond additions as graph actions optimized by policy gradients under
chemical rules \citep{you2018gcpn}. Later systems incorporate chemical
feedback while improving use of expensive evaluations. Augmented Memory
combines experience replay with SMILES augmentation
\citep{guo2024augmentedmemory}, and genetic-guided GFlowNets distill an
evolutionary search policy for sample-efficient optimization
\citep{kim2024geneticgflownets}. SketchMol uses molecular-expert reinforcement
for generation and editing, whereas MolEditRL jointly optimizes property
alignment and source preservation. MolMem adds long-term exemplar and skill
memories to an agentic editing policy \citep{wang2026molmem}. Concurrent
preprints explore group-relative alignment for multi-objective language models
and multi-step tool-using edit agents \citep{gao2026cmoral,yang2025molact}.
MolProgram uses the verifier differently from an inference-time search method:
it standardizes outcome rewards only within candidate groups sampled from one
training request and updates the same optional-source policy for both regimes.
We separately report raw-prefix editing and the property-selected de novo
protocol. This follows the broader lesson that optimization quality depends on
the allowed evaluation budget \citep{gao2022sampleefficiency}, and prevents
property-aware selection from being conflated with improvement of the molecular
policy itself.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The experiments establish weight sharing, not universal task improvement. The
pre-RL checkpoint improves editing at small sampling budgets but gives up
two- and three-property de novo success relative to the strong anti-copy
continuation; the four-property stratum improves modestly. The hard 6p/7p
audit is small and shows no overall strict advantage. Moreover, the reported
editing and de novo sets contain 100 conditions per task or stratum, so they
are frozen pilot estimates rather than full-benchmark replacements. Finally,
the property verifiers and the de novo finalizer remain external. The
group-relative continuation is deliberately small and critic-free; its locked
single-seed comparison can identify a signal but cannot establish robustness.
Future work should scale the evaluation and training groups, add independent
seeds, and study stronger drift control while retaining the same
single-checkpoint contract.

\section{Conclusion}
MolProgram shows that molecular construction and source-conditioned editing
can share one causal language-model checkpoint and one strict interface. A
validity-aware multi-negative objective improves editing without introducing a
router or mode-specific head, while a verifier-aligned group-relative update
directly targets the gap between raw and best-of-many success. The completed
pre-RL results reveal measurable interference rather than uniform transfer;
the locked post-training comparison determines whether relative outcome
feedback reduces that interference. This explicit boundary makes
single-checkpoint molecular design a testable step toward a more fully unified
system.
