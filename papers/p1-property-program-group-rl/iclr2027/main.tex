\begin{abstract}
Goal-directed molecular design is central to drug discovery and materials
development, where a request may combine several numerical objectives and
require either constructing a new molecule or modifying an existing one.
Current systems commonly treat these regimes separately, express multiple
objectives as flat vectors or unconstrained text, and evaluate them through
property-selected best-of-many outputs. We introduce \emph{MolProgram}, a
unified molecular design framework in which each request is compiled into a
variable-length program of property identities, targets, tolerances, and
directions. MolProgram shares the program representation, programmatic
verifier, and group-relative reinforcement principle across two
state-appropriate transition operators: an empty initial graph invokes a
direct SMILES constructor, whereas a populated graph invokes a source-anchored
graph-event editor. This choice is fixed by the molecular boundary condition,
not predicted by a learned task router. The construction branch augments
explicit numerical program tokens with frozen LLM-derived instruction features
and a property-count curriculum; both branches learn from groups of raw
molecular outcomes without an external molecule library or
property-aware inference-time reranking. On two-to-seven-property de novo
programs, group-relative post-training raises Pass@8 from 35.37\% to 41.72\%
and Pass@20 from 54.88\% to 62.52\%; on out-of-distribution programs, Pass@20
rises from 35.20\% to 41.70\%. On MolEdit-Instruct, it improves strict
candidate-level accuracy from 43.35\% to 46.12\%, exceeding MolEditRL's
reported 45.0\% macro under the same criterion, with 97.51\% candidate
validity. Relaxed accuracy remains lower than MolEditRL (61.90\% versus
72.70\%), delimiting the claim. These results support unification at the level
of compositional objectives and reinforcement learning while preserving the
distinct transition geometry required by molecular construction and editing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Goal-directed molecular design is widely used in drug discovery and materials
development to construct or modify molecules toward desired physicochemical
and biological profiles. The task can be formulated as a conditional molecular
transition problem: given a property program
$P=\{(j,y_j,\tau_j,d_j)\}_{j=1}^{m}$ and an initial molecular graph $G_0$,
find a terminal graph $G_T$ that maximizes a verifier
$V(P,G_T;G_0)$ over the transition family permitted by $G_0$. Each clause in
$P$ specifies a property identity $j$, target $y_j$, tolerance $\tau_j$, and
optional direction $d_j$; $G_0=\varnothing$ defines de novo construction,
whereas $G_0=G_{\mathrm{src}}$ additionally requires source-preserving editing.
A general solution samples actions
$a_t\sim\pi_{\theta_{b}}(a_t\mid P,G_t,a_{<t})$ from a legal support
$\mathcal A_b(G_t)$, applies $G_{t+1}=\mathcal I_b(G_t,a_t)$, and stops at a
candidate molecule, where the boundary index
$b=\mathbf{1}[G_0\neq\varnothing]$ distinguishes construction from editing.

Existing approaches instantiate this iteration through several broad
paradigms. Sequence generators decode molecular strings token by token and
provide a compact route to conditional construction \citep{bagal2022molgpt}.
Graph and image diffusion models iteratively denoise structural states and can
inject numerical or categorical guidance throughout generation
\citep{liu2024graphdit,wang2025sketchmol}. Arbitrary-subset conditional models
use property masking, classifier-free guidance, or self-criticism to address
different subsets of available objectives
\citep{jolicoeurmartineau2024stggplus}. Instruction-tuned molecular language
models transfer natural-language specifications across properties and molecular
operations \citep{dey2025gellm3o,dey2025controllable}. Verifier-guided sampling
and reinforcement learning instead turn molecular predictors or deterministic
property functions into outcome-level feedback
\citep{wang2025sketchmol,gao2026cmoral,shao2024deepseekmath}.

Despite their different representations, these methods all optimize
condition-dependent molecular transitions. However, their usual interfaces
obscure the specific generalization problem. A flat property vector hides the
length and clause structure of a variable objective set, making high-order
composition difficult to separate from interpolation among correlated
properties. Free-form text is semantically expressive but underspecifies
numerical targets, tolerances, reversed directions, and extreme values. At the
operation level, direct string decoding is efficient for global construction
but fragile when most of a source molecule must be retained, whereas local
graph events preserve source structure but create an unnecessarily long horizon
from an empty state. Consequently, separate generation and editing systems
discard a shared objective language, while a universal decoder forces two
incompatible transition geometries into one action space. Large candidate
budgets and property-aware selection can then conceal these weaknesses by
increasing search rather than the probability assigned to a strict solution
\citep{gao2022sampleefficiency}.

Our key observation is that the natural locus of unification is the
compositional objective, not the surface geometry of every molecular action.
The same property program can define success for both an empty and a populated
initial graph, and the same verifier can convert groups of candidates into
relative learning signals. The initial graph already provides the necessary
boundary condition: an empty state requires global construction, while a
populated state requires local source-anchored change. Deterministically
selecting the corresponding transition family therefore preserves a unified
optimization problem without introducing a learned router or pretending that
construction and editing have identical error geometry.

Thus, we present \textbf{MolProgram}, which combines LLM-derived instruction
conditioning, explicit property-program tokens, state-appropriate molecular
transitions, and group-relative policy optimization (GRPO). MolProgram first
compiles each request into deterministic tokens for its active property clauses.
For an empty initial graph, a compact autoregressive Transformer combines these
tokens with frozen LLM-derived instruction features, emits a complete SMILES
string, and follows a property-count curriculum. For a populated graph, a
validity-preserving event kernel proposes local transitions anchored to the
source. A common programmatic interface evaluates property satisfaction and,
when applicable, source similarity; within-condition candidate groups provide
relative advantages for both operators. Source occupancy fixes the operator
before inference, and neither an LLM router nor an output-side property ranker
selects among models or candidates.

The main contributions of this work are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We formulate de novo generation and molecular editing as one
  initial-state-conditioned transition problem and introduce MolProgram, a
  framework that shares a variable-length property language, verifier contract,
  and group-relative learning principle across a direct molecular constructor
  and a source-anchored event editor. It requires neither a learned task router,
  an external molecular library, nor property-aware inference-time selection.
  \item We develop a property-count curriculum and a budget-transparent
  evaluation that separates candidate accuracy from Pass@$k$. GRPO improves
  de novo Pass@20 from 54.88\% to 62.52\% on 2p--7p programs and from 35.20\%
  to 41.70\% on OOD programs; on MolEdit-Instruct, it raises strict
  candidate-level accuracy from 43.35\% to 46.12\%, compared with MolEditRL's
  reported 45.0\%, while the relaxed-threshold deficit is reported explicitly.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews
conditional molecular generation, editing, and reinforcement post-training.
Section 3 formalizes compositional property programs and the unified molecular
transition problem. Section 4 presents MolProgram and its training objectives.
Section 5 reports de novo, editing, complexity-scaling, and
out-of-distribution results. Section 6 discusses limitations and broader
implications, and Section 7 concludes the paper.
