\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We organize the results around three questions: whether group-relative
post-training improves direct generation at practical candidate budgets,
whether the improvement persists as property programs become more
compositional, and where the present formulation fails when extended from
de novo generation to source-conditioned editing. Unless stated otherwise,
all MolProgram results use one frozen evaluation seed. Candidates are kept in
generation order: $k=1$ is the first raw draw, and Pass@$k$ measures whether
at least one of the first $k$ draws is a valid strict solution. No
property-reranked output is reported as one-shot generation.

\subsection{Group-relative post-training improves low-budget generation}
\label{sec:results-budget}

Table~\ref{tab:budget-scaling} compares SFT and Group-RL on 6,000
two-to-seven-property programs and 1,000 out-of-distribution (OOD) programs.
The central result is that the Group-RL gain is already present at small
budgets rather than appearing only under best-of-256 search. On the 2p--7p
benchmark, Group-RL increases first-draw strict success from 7.02\% to 8.93\%
and Pass@8 from 35.37\% to 41.72\%. At $k=20$, it improves both the raw strict
fraction (7.30\% to 9.41\%) and the probability of solving a program at least
once (54.88\% to 62.52\%).

The OOD result is more nuanced. SFT is slightly better on the first draw
(3.50\% versus 3.20\%), so Group-RL does not uniformly improve one-shot
generation. Once a small candidate group is available, however, Group-RL
overtakes SFT: Pass@8 rises from 19.70\% to 23.60\%, and Pass@20 from 35.20\%
to 41.70\%. Thus the post-training improvement is best understood as shifting
probability mass toward useful regions of the conditional distribution, while
leaving enough variance that its OOD benefit is clearer over small groups than
on a single draw.

\subsection{The gain persists as programs become more compositional}
\label{sec:results-complexity}

Table~\ref{tab:complexity-scaling} resolves Pass@8 and Pass@20 by the number
of active properties. Absolute success decreases monotonically with program
length for both models, confirming that the benchmark exposes a genuine
compositional difficulty axis. Nevertheless, Group-RL improves every property
count at both budgets. At $k=20$, the gains are 4.9 points for 6p and 8.4
points for 7p programs; the largest increase occurs at 5p (11.8 points).
The result therefore cannot be explained only by easier two-property
conditions dominating the aggregate.

The improvement is not simply a consequence of using a larger rollout group.
On the fixed 6p/7p subset, increasing the training group from 16 to 64 raises
raw validity but reduces Pass@20 from 50.78\% to 45.31\%, driven by a 7p drop
from 50.78\% to 39.84\%. We retain the group-16 policy and treat the group-64
run as a negative ablation: useful group-relative learning depends on the
training distribution and regularization, not only on the number of sampled
rollouts.

\subsection{Matched-budget comparison with an image-based generator}
\label{sec:results-external-denovo}

We next compare with the reported SketchMol result under a matched budget of
40 candidates per condition. Table~\ref{tab:sketchmol-fair40} shows a clear
regime split. MolProgram is stronger on low-order programs, improving 2p by
11.3 points and 3p by 6.4 points, and is within 0.9 points at 4p. SketchMol is
stronger from 5p onward and leads the overall average by 5.0 points. This is
not a state-of-the-art result; rather, it localizes the remaining gap to the
high-order regime that motivates property-count curricula and verifier-based
post-training. Importantly, MolProgram produces SMILES directly and does not
retrieve or materialize molecules from an external candidate library.

\subsection{Raw validity, rather than uniqueness, limits sample efficiency}
\label{sec:results-validity}

The budget curves expose a limitation hidden by selected-molecule validity.
On 2p--7p, only 29.2\% of raw SFT draws and 33.5\% of raw Group-RL draws are
valid at $k=1$; on OOD, raw validity is approximately 12\% for both models.
Nearly every valid draw is unique, so the failure is not conventional mode
collapse. Instead, most samples are nonempty strings that RDKit cannot parse.
Group-RL improves in-distribution validity by about five points, but it does
not repair OOD syntax. Consequently, high best-of-many validity should not be
interpreted as a high probability of producing a valid molecule in one
attempt.

\subsection{Source-conditioned editing reveals the boundary of direct decoding}
\label{sec:results-editing}

We finally test whether the same direct decoder can serve as a molecular
editor. Table~\ref{tab:moledit-table1} gives the matched MolEdit-Instruct
result. Direct Group-RL produces a valid first candidate only 23.4\% of the
time and obtains zero accuracy at the strict 0.65 similarity threshold. A
20-candidate property-aware selector improves relaxed accuracy to 19.0\%, but
remains far below the reported MolEditRL result and is explicitly an assisted,
not one-shot, protocol. Group-relative post-training alone therefore does not
turn a de novo SMILES decoder into a source-preserving editor.

This failure is architectural rather than a reward-weight issue. A
source-similarity reward applied to direct SMILES Group-RL previously left
the fraction above the similarity threshold at zero. In contrast, replacing
free-form editing with executable GraphEditDSL actions changes reachability.
On a 200-example paired validation set, the protected action policy raises raw
$k=1$ validity from 31.0\% to 100\% and
$\operatorname{Acc}_{\mathrm{all}}(.65)$ from 0\% to 20.0\%, without reducing
held-out de novo strict success. At $k=8$, an explicitly assisted finalizer
reaches 43.5\% strict accuracy. These validation results support a shared
property-program interface, but we reserve a full external editing claim until
the source-consistency gate and frozen test evaluation are complete.

The same distinction appears on MuMOInstruct. Under the official
20-candidate oracle, direct Group-RL attains 50.4\% property success, including
32.3\% on in-distribution tasks and 68.8\% on OOD tasks. However, the mean
source similarity of successful outputs is only 0.098 and no input satisfies
the additional $0.4$ source-similarity diagnostic. The model can find molecules
with the requested properties, but it does so largely by generating a new
molecule rather than editing the source. This separates two capabilities that
an aggregate property score can conflate: compositional property control and
source-preserving transformation.
